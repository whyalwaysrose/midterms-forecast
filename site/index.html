What you are looking at The Senate in ninety seconds — and why most of the map is grey

The Senate

One hundred seats — two per state, regardless of size. California has 39 million people and two senators; Wyoming has 590,000 and also two. Terms run six years.

Which is why the map misleads if you read it by area: Wyoming looks about sixty times the size of Rhode Island and is worth exactly the same, one seat.

Why most states are grey

Terms are staggered so the chamber never turns over at once. Seats sit in three groups and only one group faces voters every two years.

2026 is that group's turn — 33 seats — plus special elections in Florida and Ohio, whose senators left mid-term for Secretary of State and Vice President. Hence 35 contests. The other 65 senators aren't on any ballot this year.

What winning means

Democrats need 51 seats; Republicans need 50 — the Vice President breaks ties, and JD Vance is a Republican. Republicans hold 53 now. Democrats keep 34 seats that aren't up, so they need 17 of the 35.

A majority sets the agenda and confirms judges and cabinet posts on a simple majority. Most other legislation still needs 60 votes to break a filibuster, so a narrow win is real but well short of a free hand.

Why midterms are different

2026 falls halfway through the presidential term. The president's party usually loses ground — though the Senate is less reliable about that than the House, since which seats happen to be up matters as much as the national mood.

A probability isn't a prediction: a 30% chance should come in about three times in ten. Being surprised is part of the design.

How this forecast is made

The short version: a Bayesian model estimates where each race stands from the polls, then adds the amount that polls have historically been wrong by. Both halves are described below — including how the second one was measured rather than guessed.

One equation

Everything is tracked on the log-odds of the Democratic share of the two-party vote. Each race, on each day, is:

θr,t = αr + λr·ηt + εr,t

ηt
The national environment — a random walk driven by generic-ballot polls. One number for the whole country on a given day.
αr
The race’s baseline if the country were exactly tied, from presidential lean in 2024 and 2020 plus incumbency.
λr
Elasticity — how hard this race swings when the country swings. Estimated per race, pooled toward 1.
εr,t
The race’s own drift: candidate news, spending, scandal.

Why it is built this way

Defining the baseline at a tied national environment is what stops the national swing being counted twice — once inside each race’s starting point and again through η.

Race drift is correlated across similar states. If Michigan moves toward Democrats, Wisconsin probably did too, and treating them as independent throws that away. This matters most for the unpolled races, which would otherwise have nothing to follow but the national trend.

The model is fitted with PyMC using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, so the output is a full probability distribution rather than a single number. The seat forecast comes from simulating all 35 races together many times.

Where the polls come from

Every poll is pulled from the VoteHub Polling API, which aggregates hundreds of pollsters and publishes under a Creative Commons licence. This run used — state polls and — generic-ballot polls from — pollsters.

Polls are dated to the middle of their field period rather than their publication date: a poll tells you about opinion while it was being taken.

One survey, one poll

A pollster testing several possible matchups publishes several rows, and they are not several polls — they are one sample of one set of people, asked more than one question. One Michigan survey of 1,163 voters arrived as five rows, three of them naming Democrats who lost the primary. Counting those separately both overstates how much evidence there is and credits a party with a candidate who is not on the ballot.

Each survey is therefore reduced to a single reading, preferring the matchup that is actually happening. Who that is comes from the polls themselves: once a primary is settled, pollsters stop testing the losers. Where a primary genuinely has not happened yet, the readings are averaged rather than chosen between.

This is why a race can show fewer polls than you might expect. Maine counts three, not thirty-four: its nominee changed in July, and every earlier survey there measured a contest between different people.

What gets thrown away

Primary polls, polls older than the window, samples under 100, and any poll that cannot be reduced to a single Democrat-versus-Republican matchup. Candidate names carry no party in the feed, so an explicit roster maps them — and when a name is unrecognised the poll is skipped rather than guessed at. A wrong party assignment would corrupt a race’s entire history; a skipped poll only loses information.

How each poll is adjusted

Polls are not neutral instruments, so the model estimates and removes three biases before believing them:

  • House effects. Each frequent pollster gets its own persistent lean, constrained to average out across all pollsters — otherwise “everyone leans Democratic” and “the race is more Democratic” would be indistinguishable.
  • Voter screen. Registered-voter and adult samples are corrected relative to likely voters.
  • Partisan sponsorship. Polls released by campaigns and aligned groups are corrected back toward the centre.

A pollster needs at least three polls before earning its own house effect. Give a one-off pollster a free parameter and it simply absorbs that poll’s own noise, which makes every individual poll look more informative than it is.

Polls are wrong together, not separately

This is the part that decides whether the headline number is honest.

If all 35 races could err independently, those errors would cancel across the map and the seat forecast would be absurdly confident. They do not. When polls miss in Ohio they tend to miss the same way in Iowa. So on top of the model’s estimate, the simulation adds two errors:

  • A national miss of about — points, applied identically to every race. This is what fattens the tails of the seat distribution.
  • A state-specific miss of about — points, correlated between states that vote alike and only partly cancelling across the map.

Together, roughly — points of margin per race — which is why even a comfortable-looking lead is not a safe seat.

Does a 70% call win 70% of the time?

A forecast that says 70% and is right 95% of the time is just as broken as one that is right 40% of the time. So the model is scored against — real Senate races from — past cycles, where the answer is known.

Two different tests

Against past elections. Brier score — — an improvement of — on simply calling every race for whoever leads the polls. How often the true result landed inside each interval:

Against polls it has not seen. The model is also refit on older polls and asked to predict recent ones it was never shown. That catches a different failure: a model that has learned this cycle’s noise rather than its signal.

Neither test can prove the 2026 forecast is right. They establish that this method has been honest about its own uncertainty in the past, which is the most anyone can claim before an election happens.

What this cannot do

  • It cannot know whether the polls are biased this year. The error model is calibrated to how wrong polls have been on average across past cycles. A miss larger than anything in that record would not be anticipated here.
  • Races with no polling rest on fundamentals alone. Their centres come from presidential lean and incumbency, which know nothing about candidate quality, fundraising or scandal. Their intervals are wide for good reason.
  • Nebraska is a special case. Dan Osborn runs as an independent and has said he would not automatically caucus with either party. Chamber arithmetic forces a side, so he is counted with the Democrats. Read that race, and its contribution to the seat total, with the caveat in mind.

What a probability means

A 30% chance is not a prediction that something will not happen. It is a claim that in roughly three cycles out of ten, it does. The reliability table under Testing is the evidence that these particular probabilities behave that way.
